# Learning ICT, not memorising summaries

These notes are written for an Upper Sixth learner who needs to understand a topic, use it and explain a decision—not merely recognise a definition. You should already be able to manage files, use basic application software and describe the main parts of a computer. Whenever a new technical term is introduced, its meaning is explained before it is used to solve a problem.

Our running example is **Mboa Community College**, a fictional school near Yaoundé. The school has a computer room, an administration office and a small library. Its learners also work with imagined clinics, traders and farming cooperatives. These scenarios are teaching examples, not claims that a particular real organisation uses a particular technology.

## How to work through a lesson

Read the opening situation first and try to answer its question using what you already know. Then read the explanation and follow the worked example without skipping its reasoning. A worked example shows how to move from a problem to an answer; it is not just another fact to memorise. Redraw the labelled diagrams and explain each arrow aloud. An arrow normally represents a connection, a direction of communication or a step in a process, not decoration.

Complete the guided activity before attempting the questions independently. Write your answers before consulting the answer guidance. That guidance explains the important reasoning; it is not an official GCE marking scheme. If your answer differs, ask whether it is technically correct and supported by the stated situation. Several design questions have more than one defensible answer.

A useful habit is to keep an ICT notebook with three sections: explanations in your own words; practical evidence such as a test result, screenshot or saved file; and corrections explaining why your first attempt was wrong. A list of successful clicks is less informative than an explanation of what those clicks achieved.

## What a good Upper Sixth answer does

**Define** means give the precise meaning of a term. **Describe** means give its relevant features or steps. **Explain** means show how or why something happens. **Compare** means discuss similarities and differences on the same criteria. **Evaluate** means weigh strengths and limitations against the needs in the question, then reach a supported judgement. **Demonstrate** means perform a task and provide evidence that it worked.

For example, “Use fibre because it is good” is not an explanation. “Use fibre between the two buildings because the route exceeds the usual 100 m copper Ethernet channel limit and fibre is not affected by electrical interference; allow for the higher installation cost” is a reasoned recommendation. You will practise this kind of connection between a fact and a consequence throughout the term.

The GCE Board describes Advanced Level assessment as involving thinking, application, synthesis and evaluation, not recall alone [S2]. The earlier MINESEC high-school ICT syllabus also emphasises fundamental principles, practical competence and informed decisions in real-life situations [S3]. These notes therefore combine technical explanations, safe practical work and discussion of social consequences. The supplied 2026–2027 progression sheet, not the older syllabus, determines this book's first-term lesson coverage [S1].

## Safe and inclusive practical work

Use a teacher-approved laboratory, test accounts and fictional data. Do not publish learners' personal details, scan networks you do not own, disable security protections to make a task work or make a real payment for a classroom exercise. Ask permission before photographing, recording or sharing another person's work. A practical is successful only if it works **and** respects safety and privacy.

Where equipment or connectivity is unavailable, use the paper alternative to understand the process, then complete the real practical when supervised equipment becomes available. A paper simulation is useful preparation, but it is not evidence that you can configure a real network or send an actual attachment. Work in rotating groups so that each learner performs the key actions rather than watching one confident classmate do everything.

## Scope of this book

The book follows Lessons 1–46, including the nine integration activities, across Weeks 1–13 of the supplied ICT progression. It includes two locally authored evaluations and remediation activities at the points shown in the sheet. The stated weekly workload is eight periods; the school determines the length of a period and the detailed timetable. Part headings in this book organise the reading and are not new official syllabus modules.

First-term HTML covers document structure and basic content. CSS, JavaScript, hosting and the later-term topics are deliberately not taught here. The source and alignment notes at the end explain an inconsistent class label on the supplied PDF and distinguish official source information from teaching examples.
